## The five most significant fixes

1. **The length cue across the whole bank is gone.** In the draft, the correct option was the longest in 41 of 79 items, against roughly 20 by chance. In 21 items it was noticeably longer than every distractor (by more than 8%), so a student could score well just by picking the longest option. Examples are u1-q20 (66% longer), u1-q18 (31%), u1-q09, u1-q35 and u2-q09.
   - Now no option stands out by length in any direction: correct option noticeably longest 0 (was 21), noticeably shortest 0 (was 2), a distractor noticeably longest 0 (was 7).
   - I trimmed answers and balanced distractors without adding hedges. Where a correct answer was hedged ("Not necessarily" in u1-q35, "…but an arbitrary transfer can be challenged" in u2-q21), it was made decisive or a distractor was given a matching qualifier.

2. **u2-q19 had two defensible answers and overstated the law (flagged point b).** It asked which part of a retrospective Finance Act was "open to challenge", but the retrospective tax in part (a) can itself be tested for reasonableness under Rai Ramkrishna. The stem now asks which part the Constitution forbids from operating retrospectively. The explanation no longer says that "penal provisions" in general cannot be retrospective; it is limited to criminal liability under Article 20(1).

3. **u2-q31 had a distractor that is correct under today's law (flagged point c).** "Where cost cannot be found it is treated as nil" is what Parliament later enacted for self-generated goodwill. It was replaced with a different misconception (that the gain moves to another head). The stem now says the Act deems no cost. Both u2-q31 and card u2-k13 now note that the legislature later filled the gap.

4. **u1-q35 rested on a doubtful example and a misattributed authority.** It used a liquor licence fee as the model of a regulatory fee. Liquor licence fees are usually analysed as the price of a State privilege, so the example was changed to a security-agency licence. The answer "Not necessarily" was made decisive, and the regulatory-fee rule is no longer credited to Liberty Cinema.

5. **Two near-duplicate pairs were rewritten.**
   - u2-q26 and u2-q23 both resolved to "horizontal". u2-q26 now tests the vertical share: why reorganising a State into Union territories cut the States' share from 42% to 41%.
   - u1-q33 and u1-q34 shared the same market scenario and the same "exact correlation" distractor. u1-q33 now tests compulsion and credit to the Consolidated Fund.

## Inaccuracies and divergences flagged in the notes

The items follow the notes throughout. Where the notes are contested, incomplete or wrong, no item or card depends on the doubtful point.

Flags (a) to (d) are the author's. The rest are mine and come from my own knowledge; I did not check them against the reports while reviewing. This applies especially to (e), (f), (h), (i) and (k). Check each against the reported judgment or the bare text of the Constitution or the Act before amending the notes.

- **(a) s9, "a monopolist shifts tax more easily than a firm in a competitive market".** Standard theory does not support this as a general rule.
  - With constant costs, a competitive industry passes the whole tax on in the long run.
  - A monopolist with linear demand passes on only half. With constant-elasticity demand it can pass on more than the tax.
  - Suggested wording: "market structure affects how far a tax is shifted." Card u1-k13 now says this.
- **(b) s28, "penal provisions cannot be applied retrospectively".** Article 20(1) bars retrospective criminal liability and heavier punishment for an offence. It does not necessarily cover civil penalties or interest. u2-q19 uses a criminal offence, and no item relies on civil penalties.
- **(c) s38, B.C. Srinivasa Setty.** The notes do not mention that Parliament later deemed the cost of self-generated goodwill to be nil (s 55(2)(a) of the 1961 Act), which made such gains taxable by statute. u2-q31 and u2-k13 note this without citing a 2025 Act section, because the notes give none.
- **(d) s42, UCO Bank and Ratan Melting.** The notes set two propositions side by side without reconciling them:
  - circulars bind the department "even when they relax the law" (UCO Bank);
  - "a circular contrary to the statute cannot prevail" (Ratan Melting).

  The tension matters only for circulars that favour the assessee, and no item tests that case.
- **(e) s7, McDowell.** The notes credit Chinnappa Reddy J. with "colourable devices cannot be part of tax planning". That passage is in Ranganath Misra J.'s majority judgment. Chinnappa Reddy J. wrote a separate, broader concurrence, which Azadi Bachao later treated as his own view. No item names the judge.
- **(f) s8, Binoy Viswam.** That case upheld the PAN–Aadhaar link under Articles 14 and 19(1)(g) but left the Article 21 privacy challenge open. The Aadhaar judgment ((2019) 1 SCC 1) decided the privacy point. u2-q20 now cites the Aadhaar judgment.
- **(g) s16, recovery of tax collected without authority of law.** In practice recovery is subject to limitation and unjust enrichment (Mafatlal Industries v. Union of India, 1997). u2-q02 keeps the notes' wording ("can be recovered"). It is worth adding this caveat to the notes.
- **(h) s20, Article 276.** The notes paraphrase the Rs. 2,500 cap as applying to the total paid "to a State and its local bodies". The text reads "to the State or to any one municipality … or other local authority", which can be read as a cap for each authority separately. No item tests this.
- **(i) s12, regulatory fees.** Liquor licence fees are usually treated as the price of a State privilege (Har Shankar v. Deputy Excise and Taxation Commissioner, 1975), not as regulatory fees. Avoid liquor as an example of a regulatory fee.
- **(j) s33, the Sixteenth Finance Commission.** I could not independently verify the report dates or the 41% figure, and no item tests them.
- **(k) s8, the agricultural income "exemption".** Schedule II, Sl. No. 1 reflects the constitutional exclusion of agricultural income from the Union's power (Entry 82 of List I), not a policy choice by the Union. So "every exemption shifts the burden onto others" holds for the tax system as a whole, not as revenue the Union gave up. u1-q47 is worded to fit this, and its explanation says so.

## Validation

All checks were run with node on the final file:
- the JSON is valid, in the original format (2-space indent and a trailing newline);
- every quiz item has `id`, `u`, `concept`, `slide`, `q`, `ok`, `no` and `why`;
- every `no` has 3 unique, non-empty strings, none equal to `ok`;
- all ids are unique and follow the `uN-qNN` / `uN-kNN` pattern;
- every `slide` exists in `study-data.json` and belongs to the item's own unit;
- every `u` is `u1` or `u2`, and every card `k` is `case` or `concept`;
- every `concept` label is 8 words or fewer, and every explanation is 2 to 4 sentences of 90 words or fewer;
- every card back is 60 words or fewer, and every case card ends with its authority;
- quiz fields contain no HTML, since `why` renders as plain text.

Result: 0 errors and 0 warnings.
